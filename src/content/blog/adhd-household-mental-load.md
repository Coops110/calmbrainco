Ask anyone running a household with ADHD what the hard part is, and it's rarely the big, visible jobs. It's the hundreds of small things nobody assigned to anyone, that exist only in one person's head.

## Is the problem forgetfulness

No. The things that fall through tend to be the exact things nobody could have "remembered better," because they were never a task on a list in the first place. Knowing the pediatrician's number, knowing a permission slip is due, knowing which kid will eat which dinner: none of that was ever written down anywhere another person could see it. A good memory doesn't fix a system where only one person has access to the information.

## Why does the load concentrate on one person

Once one person becomes the household's default place to look things up, every new piece of information defaults to them too, and the gap between what they're carrying and what's actually written down anywhere else keeps growing. Nobody decided this on purpose. It's just what happens when a household has zero shared pages and one very reliable head.

## What should a shared system assume

A workable system assumes the household, not one person, is the unit that needs the information: a form a partner or sitter could actually read, a chore list that gets reassigned instead of memorized, a contact sheet that doesn't live only in one phone.

## How do you make the load visible in practice

Write the invisible parts down somewhere everyone in the household can see, not just the parts that look like tasks. A mental load dump catches "I'm the only one who knows the school nurse's number" the same way a brain dump catches "I keep forgetting to call the dentist." Pair it with a shared weekly view so where everyone needs to be isn't locked in one person's head either.

## One shared system or several separate ones

Either can work. The point here isn't that a household needs one single binder; it's that the information needs to exist somewhere other than one person's memory, in whatever format a household actually uses.
