A spreadsheet is a perfectly good tool for tracking money, right up until the moment you forget it exists. That is not a discipline problem. It is what happens when a system depends entirely on remembering to open it, with no physical trigger sitting in the room with you.

## Is the problem the maths

No. Most budgeting advice assumes the hard part is arithmetic: categorising a purchase, running a total, checking it against a limit. The arithmetic was never the problem. The problem is that a spreadsheet lives behind a login, on a device, in a tab that gets closed, and once it is out of sight it is out of mind until a bank notification says something has gone wrong.

## Why does paper stay visible when a file does not

A printed page sitting on a desk or tucked into a planner does not need to be opened, loaded, or remembered as a separate app. It is just there. That is the advantage: not that paper is more powerful than software, but that it does not depend on a habit that is specifically hard to sustain, checking in on something nobody reminded you to check.

## What should a realistic ADHD budget assume

A workable system assumes some weeks get fully logged and some do not, some categories are estimates and some are left blank, and none of that means the system failed.

## How do you budget with ADHD in practice

A simple monthly routine: look at the overview page once a month, add to the weekly spending log when you remember, and check the bills page when a bill arrives rather than on a fixed schedule.

## Spreadsheet, app or paper

All three have trade-offs; a hybrid is fine. The case here is only that paper solves the visibility problem that spreadsheets and apps both share, since none of them are open by default.
