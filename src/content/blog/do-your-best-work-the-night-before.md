Standard school advice says start early, work a little every day, and finish with time to spare. For a lot of ADHD brains, that advice doesn't fail because of laziness. It fails because starting early on a task with a distant deadline can be close to impossible, and starting the night before, with the deadline right there, is sometimes the only version of the task the brain will actually engage with.

## Is this just procrastination

Not in the usual sense. Ordinary procrastination is putting something off that you could start anytime. For a lot of ADHD brains, a distant deadline doesn't register as real yet, so there's nothing to resist starting: there's just nothing pulling focus toward the task at all. Pressure close to a deadline changes that completely, which is why the same task that was untouchable a week out can suddenly move fast the night before.

## Why doesn't starting early work

Starting early assumes the hard part is self-control: begin now, and willpower carries you to the finish line in small daily pieces. For a brain that needs a deadline's pressure to engage, there's no amount of willpower that manufactures urgency a week in advance. Forcing an early start on a task the brain isn't engaging with usually produces thirty minutes of staring at a blank page, not real progress, and then guilt about the thirty minutes.

## So is a late start the plan

Not quite. The honest middle ground is breaking the work down so a late start is still a real plan instead of an emergency. A big project with one deadline and no structure in between is the version that goes badly at the last minute. A big project broken into a next tiny step, worked on close to the pressure that makes starting possible, tends to go better, because there's already a known first move instead of a blank page and a clock.

## What does this look like in practice

Breaking a big assignment into the next tiny step instead of the whole project. Keeping a running countdown in your own numbers instead of a syllabus date that feels far away until it suddenly isn't. Treating a night-before push as a known part of how some tasks get done, not a failure to plan better next time.
