Most habit trackers are built around one idea: an unbroken row of checkmarks. Miss a single day and the row breaks, the streak resets to zero, and the whole system starts sending a quiet message — you failed, start over.

For an ADHD brain, that message lands harder and more often than it should.

## The streak is the bug, not the feature

Streak-based tracking assumes missing a day is rare and worth punishing. But ADHD makes consistency genuinely harder — not from a lack of wanting to, but because executive function has bad days the tracker doesn't account for. When the format treats a bad day as a failure instead of just a bad day, most people don't try again tomorrow. They close the tracker and don't open it again.

That's not a willpower problem. It's a tracker that was never built for how ADHD actually works.

## What to track instead

A habit tracker that actually helps drops the streak entirely. Each day stands on its own: did you do it or not, no chain to protect, no zero to explain. Skipping Tuesday doesn't cost you Wednesday. That one change — removing the penalty for being human — is usually the difference between a tracker that gets used for a week and one that gets used for months.
