Open almost any productivity planner and you will find the same layout: a column of hours, 6am to 10pm, each one waiting for a task. It is a clean system on paper. In practice, for a lot of ADHD brains, it tends to fall apart by 9:15am.

## What goes wrong with hourly time blocking

Hourly time blocking assumes a few things that do not hold up for many ADHD brains: that time is felt consistently minute to minute, that a task takes the length you guessed, and that finishing early or late does not derail the rest of the day. When any one of those assumptions breaks, and they break often, the whole schedule cascades into crossed-out times, and the day starts to feel like a failure by 10am.

## Is it a discipline problem?

No. It is a format problem. The format asks for a kind of time perception and task estimation that is specifically hard for an ADHD brain, then treats the resulting mismatch as a personal failure. Changing the format removes the mismatch; no amount of trying harder at the old format does.

## The alternative: morning, afternoon, evening

Instead of an hour-by-hour grid, split the day into three chunks: morning, afternoon, evening. Each one is loose enough to absorb the normal shape of a day, a task running long, a slow start, without the whole plan collapsing. An hourly planner asks "what am I doing at 2pm?" A three-chunk planner asks "what does my afternoon need to hold?" The second question survives contact with an actual day.

## Why cap the day at three priorities

The other piece that matters: naming three priorities for the day, not a running list of everything. A long to-do list looks productive and functions as a guilt generator, since most of it will not get done and what is left by evening reads as a list of failures instead of the day's real work. Three items, chosen on purpose, is a list that can actually be finished.

## Does time blocking ever help with ADHD?

Sometimes, in a smaller dose: one fixed appointment anchored to a real time can be useful as a loose waypoint. The objection here is to sixteen slots covering a whole day, not to a single anchor.
