Open almost any productivity planner and you'll find the same layout: a column of hours, 6am to 10pm, each one waiting for a task. It's a clean system — on paper. In practice, for an ADHD brain, it tends to fall apart by 9:15am.

## The problem isn't discipline

Hourly time-blocking assumes a few things that don't hold up for a lot of ADHD brains: that time is felt consistently minute to minute, that a task will actually take the length you guessed, and that finishing early or late doesn't derail the rest of the day. When any one of those assumptions breaks — and for ADHD brains, they break constantly — the whole schedule cascades into a mess of crossed-out times, and the day starts to feel like a failure by 10am.

That's not a discipline problem. It's a format problem.

## What actually holds up: three chunks, not twelve hours

Instead of an hour-by-hour grid, try splitting the day into three real chunks: morning, afternoon, evening. Each one is loose enough to absorb the normal chaos of an ADHD day — a task running long, a hyperfocus session eating an hour, a slow start — without the whole plan collapsing.

The difference is subtle but real: an hourly planner asks "what am I doing at 2pm?" A three-chunk planner asks "what's the shape of my afternoon?" The second question survives contact with an actual day.

## Pair it with a hard priority cap

The other piece that matters: naming just 3 priorities for the day, not a running list of everything. A long to-do list looks productive and functions as a guilt generator — most of it won't get done, and what's left staring back at you by evening is a list of failures instead of a day's real work. Three items, chosen on purpose, is a list that can actually be finished.
